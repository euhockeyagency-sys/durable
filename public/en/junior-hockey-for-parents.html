Four questions before a move

Safety, school, budget and the family's interests

1. Safety and daily life

A teenager abroad usually lives in one of three set-ups. Ask which one, in writing, before agreeing anything.

Set-upWhat it isWhat to check
Billet (host) familyThe player lives in a local family's home; common in North American junior hockey and used by some European clubs.How the family is selected and screened, who pays them, house rules, meals, transport
Academy or school boarding houseA dormitory with staff, often linked to a school.Staff on duty at night and weekends, room sharing, study time, curfew
FlatA rented flat, alone or with teammates; more common for older juniors.Who is the responsible adult nearby, who pays rent and bills, who cooks
  • Who is legally and practically responsible for the teenager on site?
  • Who checks the daily routine, sleep and meals, and how do they report to you?
  • How does the player get to school and the rink, and how long does it take?
  • Who do you and the player call in an emergency — day and night?
  • How can the housing be changed if there is a problem, and who decides?

National rules differ: Hockey Canada's billeting package, for example, sets screening of household members aged 19 and over and an in-home interview (Hockey Canada ↗). Do not assume a European club follows the same standard — ask. More: billet family: rights, duties and safety.

2. Education

  • Enrolment: a named school and written confirmation of the place; a club invitation is not enrolment.
  • Language of teaching and whether there is language support.
  • Timetable: does it fit morning ice, away games and tournaments?
  • Recognition: how grades and the final certificate will be recognised at home.
  • Plan B: what happens to school if the player changes team mid-season.

Where each option fits: a local school when the family moves or the player is enrolled with a residence basis; a sport school or academy school when hockey and classes are planned together; online school to keep the home curriculum (check whether the host country accepts it for a residence permit — Sweden, for example, does not for non-EU minors); college or university for players finishing secondary school, especially on the North American route; language courses as preparation, not as a replacement for school. More: school and education abroad.

3. Budget

Two models: programmes that cover part of the costs or offer a stipend (for example the USHL's Tier I model covers hockey, equipment, billet housing and team travel) and pay-to-play academies and clubs where the family pays a fee. Even with no team fee the family may still pay for school, insurance, flights home, personal equipment, phone and pocket money.

Ask for every payment in writing:

  • team or programme fee and what it includes;
  • housing and meals, and who pays the host family;
  • school fees, books, uniform;
  • insurance (health and accident) and who pays first;
  • equipment, sticks and skate sharpening;
  • team travel, tournaments and camps;
  • flights home and visa or permit fees;
  • deposit: amount, when it is due, whether it is refundable;
  • refund and exit: what happens to money paid if the player is cut, injured, homesick or not registered.

4. Protecting the family's interests

  • Check the club or academy: legal entity, who receives the money, federation membership, the team the player will be registered with.
  • Get the contract or offer in a language you understand, with the season, fees, housing, school and exit terms.
  • Check who signs: for a minor, a parent or legal guardian is usually involved, but how consent and signatures work depends on the country and the programme.

Examples of how rules differ: in Czechia players under 15 need a notarised guardian signature on domestic transfer forms (ČSLH ↗); Finland requires a guardian to move permanently with a player under 18 for a permanent transfer (SJL §6.7 ↗); Sweden asks for documented representation by a guardian or appointed representative (SIF §4:7 ↗); Canada requires a custodian in Canada for a child aged 17 or under who comes to study without a parent or legal guardian, with a notarised declaration signed by the parents (IRCC ↗). There is no single rule that a parent must be present in person at signing in every country — check the federation, the programme and, for residence, the consulate.

1. Choose the route

Start with the player's birth year, position, current league, role, recent video, citizenship and objective. Then decide what you are comparing: a regular club, academy, school programme or a tryout route. League names alone do not show whether the environment fits the player.

Use the junior leagues reference and country guides to understand age categories and the structure of each market before contacting programmes. To see rules for several countries side by side, open the route comparison. Weighing a European junior team against a North American academy, junior league or the CHL? Read junior hockey in Europe vs North America for costs, visas and NCAA rules side by side. If the United States and an NCAA pathway are under consideration, review the NCDC player and parent guide for current level, age, registration, costs and eligibility checks.

2. Evaluate the conditions

For a minor, the hockey level is only one part of the decision. Ask for the school name and timetable, accommodation model, responsible adult, transport, insurance arrangements and the complete family budget. Confirm exactly what the programme provides and what the family pays separately.

For what is confirmed about school access for a foreign minor and why a school place is not a residence permit, read School and education for a young hockey player abroad.

EHA boundary: EHA can assess the sporting route and help identify questions to verify. School admission, accommodation terms, insurance, visa, residence and registration conditions must be confirmed with the relevant organisation or authority.

3. Verify an offer before committing

  • confirm the exact team, age group, coach and expected role;
  • identify whether the document is a tryout invitation, conditional offer or confirmed place;
  • get school, housing, costs, benefits and refund terms in writing;
  • confirm who handles federation registration and the IIHF transfer;
  • verify the legal entity receiving any payment and the responsible adult for the player;
  • do not treat verbal promises as confirmed conditions.

For a broader checklist, read How to verify a hockey club offer.

4. Prepare the move

The exact document list depends on country, age and programme. Common items can include passport, parental consent, school records, insurance information, accommodation confirmation and transfer documents. Use the documents guide and IIHF transfer guide, then confirm current requirements with official sources.

For the IIHF transfer steps with dates and the confirmed national documents, read Documents and the IIHF transfer for a minor hockey player; for the dates by month, the junior hockey calendar.

Red flags

Pressure to pay for a guaranteed roster place, refusal to name the team or responsible adult, unclear housing, no written education plan, travel requested before documents are checked, or terms that change after payment all require additional verification.

More detail: child safety and host-family questions, homesickness and adaptation, offer scams and an annotated sample offer, and the season cost calculator for comparing written offers.

5. Get an EHA assessment

The free first review is an initial sporting assessment. Send the player's age, position, citizenship, current team or league, Elite Prospects when available, recent video and the family's objective. EHA can identify a realistic market direction and the next step to investigate.

It is not a guarantee of selection, school admission, housing, visa, registration, transfer approval or a contract. If a deeper video review is useful, the separate full player analysis remains an optional next step rather than a required part of the process.

Frequently asked by parents

Can my child play junior hockey in Europe?

Possibly, but the realistic route depends on birth year, current level and role, recent full-game evidence, citizenship, school requirements and the rules of the target country and competition.

What can EHA assess for a junior player?

EHA can review the player's sporting profile, likely market direction and the questions a family should verify. School, accommodation, visa, registration and other official conditions must be confirmed with the relevant club, school, federation or authority.

Does an invitation mean the player has a roster place?

No. A tryout, camp invitation, conditional offer and confirmed roster place are different stages and should be verified separately.

What should parents verify before paying?

Verify the team, responsible adults, sporting role, school and housing plan, all costs and benefits, refund terms, registration and transfer responsibilities, and the legal entity receiving payment.

How much does junior hockey in Europe cost?

There is no single price. Ask for a written total covering programme fees, school, housing, meals, transport, insurance, equipment, travel, registration and transfer costs, plus refund terms.

Is this legal or immigration advice?

No. Requirements change by country and season. Official conditions must be verified with the relevant authorities and organisations.
