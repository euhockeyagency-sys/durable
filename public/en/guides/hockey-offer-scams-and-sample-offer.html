Red Flags in European Hockey: How to Spot Fake Scouts, Pay-to-Play Traps & Bad Contracts

How to tell a transparent paid service from a poor-value offer and from possible fraud, and what to check before you pay or sign.

Short answer

Before you pay or sign, check three things independently: who the person really is and whom they represent, whether the club itself confirms the offer through contact details you found yourself, and what every payment buys, who receives it and when it is refunded. An offer that guarantees a roster place or contract before anyone has assessed the player, or that demands money urgently to a personal account, needs to stop until those checks are done.

Not every paid programme is a scam. A camp, academy or agency service can be transparent and fairly priced; another can be legal but poor value; a third can show signs of fraud. This page helps you tell them apart. It is a general checklist, not legal advice for your situation.

1. Paid service, poor value or possible fraud?

Three different situations
TypeWhat it looks likeWhat to do
Transparent paid serviceA named legal entity, a written agreement that lists what you get, a price, who receives the money and refund terms. No guarantee of selection.Compare it with alternatives and decide whether it is worth the money for your goal.
Legal but poor valueEverything is in writing, but the price is high for what is delivered: for example, a showcase with few real decision-makers or a vague "marketing" package.Ask for specifics: who attends, what results previous participants had (without names), what exactly is delivered. Walk away if the answers are vague.
Signs of possible fraudMoney before any written contract, payment to a personal account, a guaranteed place, a club that cannot confirm the offer, pressure to decide within hours.Stop paying, verify independently, keep evidence, and contact your bank and the authorities if money has gone.

No single feature proves fraud. The more of the warning signs an offer has, the more independent checking it needs. This page describes patterns, not named people or organisations.

3. Check the scout or representative

Ask for the person's full name, the legal name and registration number of their company, and an email address on the company's own domain. If they say they act for a club, ask for the club's written authority and confirm it with the club. If they want to represent the player, ask for the full representation agreement before you sign anything.

Some hockey systems keep registers of agents:

  • Sweden: the players' association SICO lists certified player agents and states that only they have the right to negotiate for players with Swedish clubs.
  • Finland: the players' association SJRY publishes a code of conduct for agents; under it, an agent may offer clubs only players with whom there is a valid mandate agreement.
  • NHL: only agents certified by the NHL Players' Association may represent players in dealings with NHL clubs, and the NHLPA publishes the list.
  • Czechia: press reports describe a certification project for agents registered with the Czech federation (ČSLH). Confirm the current list with ČSLH.

Many countries have no mandatory register, so being unlisted is not proof of anything. It is a reason to ask more questions and to rely on written documents.

4. Confirm the club offer independently

  1. Find the club's official website yourself and call or email through the details published there. Ask whether the club sent this offer.
  2. Check that the club and the team or age group in the offer appear on the league's or federation's own website.
  3. Ask for the legal name, registered address and registration number of the contracting party. The name on the payment details must be the same.
  4. Ask who handles the IIHF transfer, the national registration and any residence or work permit, and when. See the IIHF transfer guide.
  5. Use the full 15-point offer check before you sign.

5. Promises that need extra checking

  • "Guaranteed" roster place or contract. Coaches select players; a registration or transfer can be refused. Ask who has seen your game and who decides.
  • "Scouts from top leagues will be there." Ask which organisations, whether they confirmed in writing, and how many players were signed after previous events.
  • "Visa guaranteed." Only the migration authority decides on a permit. A private person cannot guarantee one.
  • Salary without detail. Ask whether the figure is gross or net, monthly or for the season, and who pays housing, car and insurance.
  • "Everything is included." Ask for each item in writing with the provider and the amount.

Why a guarantee without an assessment raises questions: a real club signs a player after looking at video, statistics or a tryout and after checking that the player fits its roster and import rules. A guarantee given before anyone has seen the player means either that the document is not a real contract, or that what is being sold is a paid programme presented as a selection.

6. Payments, services and refunds

Every payment should be described in writing before you pay. For each one, ask:

Payments to describe in advance
PaymentQuestions to ask
Agency or service feeWhat exactly is delivered, by when, and what happens if nothing is delivered? Is the fee due only when a contract is signed?
Camp, showcase or tryout feeIs it a selection for a roster or a paid camp? How many roster places exist? What is refunded if the event is cancelled?
Tuition, academy or programme feeWhat does it include (school, ice, travel, equipment), who is the legal entity, and what is the refund policy if the player leaves?
Housing deposit or rentAddress, landlord or host family, what the deposit covers and when it is returned.
Visa or permit feesOfficial fees are set by the authority; ask for the official fee schedule and who pays them.
Transfer-related paymentsUnder IIHF rules no transfer fee applies to a player under 18; the current federation may charge at most CHF 300 for approval. Ask who pays any fee and on what basis.

For any service agreement, check: the parties and their registration details, the list of services, the price and payment schedule, refund conditions, the term and how to end it, any exclusivity, and which law applies. If the agreement is with an agent, also check the commission and when it becomes due.

7. Housing, insurance, equipment and travel

  • Housing: address, who else lives there, who pays, and for a minor, who the responsible adult is. See child safety and host families.
  • Insurance: who provides health and accident cover, from which date, and whether it covers hockey injuries.
  • Equipment: what the club provides (sticks, skates, protective gear) and what the player buys.
  • Travel: who books and pays the flight, when, and what happens to the return ticket if the player is released.

8. If you are asked to transfer money urgently

  1. Do not pay while the deadline is running. A legitimate club or programme can wait a day for you to verify.
  2. Verify the request through contact details you found yourself, not the ones in the message.
  3. Do not pay to a personal account or through gift cards, cryptocurrency or money-transfer services.
  4. If you have already paid, contact your bank immediately and ask whether the payment can be stopped or recalled.
  5. Report it to the police or consumer-fraud authority where you live, and tell the club through its official contacts.

9. Documents and messages to keep

  • the offer and every version of the agreement;
  • emails, chat messages and the sender's profile or phone number;
  • invoices, receipts and payment confirmations;
  • notes of phone calls with dates and names;
  • screenshots of websites or social media pages, which can disappear.

10. When to get independent legal advice

This page is a general checklist. Consider a lawyer, or the players' association in the country where it exists, when:

  • the agreement is long, exclusive or has penalty or buy-out clauses;
  • the governing law or the language of the contract is one you do not know;
  • a minor is a party to the agreement;
  • the amount involved is more than you could afford to lose;
  • you have paid and the other side does not respond.

A fictional sample offer, annotated

Fictional example. Invented for illustration. “Northern Stars HC”, “Euro Elite Hockey Management” and every detail below are made up and do not refer to any real club, agency or person.

From: Euro Elite Hockey Management <euroelite.hockey@mail-example.invalid>

Dear parent,

Your son has been selected for the U18 team of Northern Stars HC.

  1. A guaranteed place on the roster for the 2027-28 season.
  2. A registration and visa guarantee fee of EUR 4,500 is payable within 48 hours to the personal account of our manager.
  3. The fee is refunded in full after the first official game.
  4. Housing and school are included. Details will be provided on arrival.
  5. Your son will travel as a tourist and the club will arrange his documents later.
  6. Please sign the attached agreement (signature page only) and return it today.

Yours sincerely, Euro Elite Hockey Management

What is wrong with each line

  1. “Guaranteed place.” No one outside the coaching staff can guarantee selection. The offer also names no league, no coach and no age-group rules, so there is nothing to verify.
  2. The fee. It is paid within 48 hours, which leaves no time to check anything, and to a person rather than to the club or a registered company. A “visa guarantee” is not something a private person can sell. Ask who exactly the contracting entity is.
  3. The refund. It depends on an event the family does not control, and no terms are given: who refunds, from which account, by what date, and what if the player is not registered.
  4. “Included … on arrival.” There is no address, provider, school name, amount or responsible adult. For a minor this is the most important gap.
  5. Tourist travel. Immigration status decides whether the player may train and play. “Later” is not a plan; ask who applies, for which permit, and before when.
  6. “Signature page only.” Signing a page without the full text means agreeing to terms you have not seen. The same-day deadline is pressure, not a requirement.

The sender's address is on a generic mail domain, not a club domain, and the letter does not name the legal entity, its registered address or the club's coach. A real offer lets you verify each of these.

FAQ

Is every fee a sign of a scam?

No. Academies, camps and agencies can charge legitimately. What matters is who the legal entity is, whether the fee and what it buys are in a written agreement, whether the money goes to that entity and what the refund terms are. A fee tied to a guaranteed roster place is a red flag.

How do I check whether a scout or agent is real?

Ask for their full name, company registration details and written authority from the club if they claim one, and confirm it with the club yourself. Where a register exists, such as SICO's list of certified agents in Sweden or the NHLPA list for the NHL, check it.

A club contacted me on social media. Is that a problem?

Not by itself, but verify the sender: find the club's official website and contact details yourself and ask whether the offer is theirs.

What should I do if I have already paid?

Contact your bank immediately to ask whether the payment can be stopped or recalled, keep every message, and report it to the police or the consumer-fraud authority where you live.
