Junior hockey in Europe or North America: USHL, NAHL, NCAA, OHL, QMJHL and WHL compared

A side-by-side guide for families of 16 to 20-year-olds: who runs each programme, who pays, what the confirmed costs and rules are, and what to check before signing.

Short answer

For a player aged 16 to 20, the European route usually means joining a club's junior team and attending local school, with costs that depend on whether the club covers housing. The North American route usually means a paid prep school or academy, a junior league with billeting, or the CHL. Published North American academy prices for 2026-27 reach about $84,000 a year for an international boarder at Shattuck-St. Mary's. Neither route guarantees development, a contract or college eligibility: what decides it is the specific team, the role your child will have, and whether the visa, school and registration all line up.

Who this guide is for

Families of players aged 16 to 20 from the USA, Canada and the CIS countries who are comparing a move to a European junior team with a North American option. It explains the structures, the confirmed costs and rules, and what to ask before signing. It is not legal or immigration advice: confirm every permit and eligibility question with the authority that decides it.

1. How the two systems are organised

In most European countries, junior teams (U18 and U20) belong to clubs that also run a senior team. A player is registered with the national federation through the club, and school is arranged separately, sometimes through a sport-school programme such as Sweden's NIU or Finland's urheilulukio. In North America, the main options are separate structures: private prep schools and academies that charge tuition, junior leagues such as the USHL, NAHL and NCDC, and the CHL in Canada, where clubs bring in European players through the Import Draft.

The practical difference for a family: in Europe you negotiate with a club, in North America often with a school or league programme. Who you negotiate with decides who pays, who is responsible for your child, and which rules apply.

USHL, NAHL, NCAA, OHL, QMJHL and WHL at a glance

Short answer for parents: the CHL (OHL, QMJHL, WHL) and the USHL are the most selective North American junior routes, and both usually cover housing; the NAHL charges no hockey tuition but housing and family costs need checking; NCAA college hockey is a university route with its own eligibility rules. Confirmed European cost items that apply to some families:

  • Finland: from 1 August 2026, upper secondary schools must charge an annual fee to third-country students who come on a study permit. Fees vary by provider; Espoo states a total fee of about €10,300.
  • Sweden: to get a residence permit for upper secondary studies, an applicant must show at least SEK 10,656 a month (2026 applications). The application fee for under-18s is SEK 750.
  • Austria: the pupil residence permit fee is €218 and the means threshold is €1,308.39 a month (2026).

5. Visa, residence and sporting registration are three separate checks

A roster spot does not give your child the right to live in the country, and a residence permit does not register them to play. Plan for three separate approvals:

  1. Entry and residence: decided by the country's migration authority, based on your child's citizenship and the purpose (usually school).
  2. School place: decided by the school; some permits depend on it.
  3. Sporting registration: the club's national federation requests the international transfer through the IIHF system.

What is confirmed for some countries:

  • Sweden: a residence permit for upper secondary school is possible only for an IB programme or an exchange through an exchange organisation. Migrationsverket does not mention hockey programmes such as NIU, so whether a non-EU minor can be admitted under another route is not confirmed.
  • Finland: the school fee must be paid before the study permit can be applied for (Espoo's guidance). Migri's under-18 requirements were not confirmed.
  • Canada: a study permit is required for a programme longer than six months; a child aged 17 or younger coming without a parent or legal guardian needs a custodian in Canada (IRCC Guide 5269, 6 October 2026).
  • USA: no visa category exists for amateur junior hockey. An F-1 student visa is tied to a SEVP-certified school, and secondary sources say online or home schooling does not qualify. EHA could not read the primary USCIS and State Department pages, so confirm with the school and the consulate.
  • Online school is not a confirmed basis for a permit in any country EHA researched. Sweden states that a permit cannot be granted for distance learning.

Details by country: school and education abroad and documents and IIHF transfer for a minor.

6. International transfer for a player under 18

Under the IIHF International Transfer Regulations, a player under 18 may transfer between federations, no transfer fee applies before the 18th birthday, and the general 1 July to 15 February deadline does not apply to under-18s. The current federation may not charge more than CHF 300 for approval. The player may not play for the new club before the transfer is approved.

National federations add their own conditions. Sweden requires documentation from the home federation and a guardian or a representative appointed by the parents. Finland requires the guardian to relocate with a player under 18 who transfers permanently. Ask the club which rule applies before you plan the move.

7. For families from the CIS

Entry rules depend on your child's citizenship and on the consulate where you apply, so check both before you commit. For players registered with the Russian or Belarusian federations, note that on 28 February 2022 the IIHF suspended Russian and Belarusian national and club teams from IIHF competitions; in May 2026 the IIHF Congress reinstated Belarus only for a few lower-tier events, including the men's U18 top division. How an individual transfer from these federations is processed is not something this page can confirm. Ask the receiving club to check with its federation before any payment or travel.

8. Development: what to compare

Neither route is better by default. Compare the specific team on the points that shape a season:

  • Expected role and ice time, and who decides it. No club can promise it in advance, but you can ask how players of your child's level were used last season.
  • Number of games and practices a week, and the level of opponents.
  • Whether the club has a senior team and how junior players move up.
  • School load and travel: an exhausting timetable costs development too.
  • Language, housing and support: adaptation affects performance.

To place your child's current level against European leagues, start with the level calculator.

9. NCAA and the CHL: what the rules say

If college hockey in the USA is a goal, the rules changed in 2025 and 2026. Check them with the NCAA Eligibility Center; EHA cannot confirm any player's eligibility.

  • Age-based model: on 23 June 2026 NCAA Division I adopted a model that allows up to five years of eligibility if a player enrols in college no later than the academic year after the 19th birthday. Players first enrolling full time in 2026-27 may use whichever rule set is more favourable; from fall 2027 only the age-based model applies. A college hockey advocacy FAQ gives 1 August 2026 as the start date; the NCAA's own page is the reference. College Hockey Inc. notes the model does not apply to Divisions II and III.
  • CHL players: from 1 August 2025, playing in the CHL no longer costs NCAA Division I eligibility, provided the player receives no more than actual and necessary expenses.
  • Professional teams: NCAA legislative records state that taking part with a professional team beyond a limited tryout jeopardises eligibility regardless of expenses. A contract with a European senior club therefore needs checking before signing, not after.
  • School records: keep official transcripts from year nine onward; non-English records need a certified word-for-word translation.

More detail: age pathway, NCAA and CHL rules.

10. Safety and daily life

Before a minor moves, know who the responsible adult is, where your child will live and how the family can be reached. Ask for the host family arrangement in writing and agree on a contact plan. See child safety and host families and homesickness and adaptation.

11. Offer checklist

Before you sign or pay, get written answers to these points:

  • Who makes the offer: a club, a school, a league programme or an intermediary? Who signs for your child?
  • Which costs the club covers and which the family pays: housing, meals, school, insurance, flights, equipment, fees.
  • Where your child will live, with whom, and who the responsible adult is.
  • Which school, whether the place is confirmed, and on what basis the residence permit is applied for.
  • Who requests the international transfer and when, and which national under-18 rule applies.
  • What is said about the team, the level and the season length, without promises of ice time.
  • How the agreement ends, what happens to fees paid, and what happens after an injury.
  • If an NCAA path matters: whether any payment exceeds actual and necessary expenses, and whether the team is professional.
  • Payment details: payments go to the organisation, not to a personal account.

Warning signs and a sample offer: offer scams and a sample offer. For a camp or tender, see camp, tender and roster place.

At 16–18: USHL, NAHL, prep schools or a European route?

Short answer: compare the specific team, not the label. The USHL and NAHL are junior leagues where hockey is not paid for by the family; prep schools combine hockey with a paid school diploma; European clubs connect juniors to their own senior team. What the step into senior hockey depends on

  • the player's role and minutes at 17–19, not the league's name;
  • physical and skating development relative to senior pace;
  • whether the club or programme has a senior team that uses its juniors;
  • import and age rules in the target league;
  • keeping NCAA eligibility open if college is the plan — check with the NCAA Eligibility Center before signing.

FAQ

Is junior hockey in Europe cheaper than in North America?

It can be, but there is no single European price. North American private academies publish tuition of up to about $84,000 a year for 2026-27. In Europe the cost depends on whether the club covers housing and whether school is free for your child's status; Finland now charges non-EU upper secondary students. Compare written offers line by line.

Does a roster spot in Europe give my child a visa?

No. Residence, school admission and sporting registration are three separate checks. The migration authority decides on residence based on citizenship and purpose, usually school.

Will playing in Europe affect NCAA eligibility?

It can. NCAA records say taking part with a professional team beyond a limited tryout jeopardises eligibility. Check any contract with the NCAA Eligibility Center before signing. EHA does not promise eligibility.

Is there a transfer fee for a player under 18?

Under the IIHF regulations, no transfer fee applies to a transfer completed before the 18th birthday, and the 1 July to 15 February window does not apply. National federations may add conditions, such as Finland's guardian relocation rule.

Can my child study online while playing in Europe?

Do not rely on it. Online study is not a confirmed basis for a residence permit in any country EHA researched, and Sweden states that a permit cannot be granted for distance learning.
